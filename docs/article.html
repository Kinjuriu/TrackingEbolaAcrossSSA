A case in Nairobi

On 3 October 2026, a man who had been ill for about a month, and who had already been treated at several hospitals in the eastern Democratic Republic of the Congo (DR Congo), travelled by road into Uganda and then flew to Nairobi, where hospital staff isolated him as soon as he arrived. The World Health Organization (WHO) reports that samples taken two days later tested positive for Bundibugyo virus, one of the three viruses known to cause large outbreaks of Ebola disease in people, and that he died the same day (WHO 2026a).

Eight more people in Kenya later tested positive, and all eight had been close to him: four were members of his family and four were health and care workers, and one of the eight died (WHO 2026a). Every one of them was already in quarantine, meaning that they were being kept apart from other people and checked each day for symptoms, by the time they fell ill, and this is why the count stopped at nine. The European Centre for Disease Prevention and Control (ECDC), the public health agency of the European Union, reports that Kenya recorded its last case on 20 October, that seven of the nine patients had recovered by 11 November, and that every person traced as a contact had finished 21 days of monitoring without falling ill (ECDC 2026).A contact is anyone who has touched a patient, their body fluids or their bedding, or shared a household or ward with them, since the patient’s symptoms began.

The containment of the Nairobi cluster did little to calm the alarm that the case set off abroad. Gulf News, citing the Saudi Press Agency, reported that on 30 October Saudi Arabia suspended visas for travellers from Kenya and barred them from entering the kingdom (Gulf News 2026),11 The suspension also covers anyone who has been in another affected country in the previous 21 days, the longest time that symptoms can take to appear. which brought the number of countries that had restricted travel from the region since May to at least twelve; the dashboard lists each restriction with its date and source.

The outbreak that the traveller carried with him is far larger than the cluster it seeded in Kenya: DR Congo’s health ministry declared it in Ituri province, in the far north-east of the country, on 15 May 2026, and WHO declared it a public health emergency of international concern, its highest level of alert, two days later (WHO 2026a; WHO 2026b). By 15 November, ECDC counted 11,376 confirmed cases and 5,568 deaths across eight of DR Congo’s provinces, which makes it the largest Ebola outbreak since the West African epidemic of 2014 to 2016 (ECDC 2026).

We began this article by asking whether a disease that has now reached a city with direct flights to the Gulf, Europe and Asia will go on to reach the rest of the world. The evidence in the sections that follow shows that it already has, in small numbers, so the more useful question is what happens after an infected person arrives somewhere new, and the answer depends on how many people each patient infects and on how quickly health workers find and isolate those patients.

An old virus

Ebola is far older than the twelve years our storyboard covers, and starting the record in 2014 can make it look like a disease that began in West Africa and moved east. The US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) and WHO both record that the virus was first identified in 1976, in two outbreaks a few months and several hundred kilometres apart: one around Yambuku, a village near the Ebola River in what is now DR Congo, where 318 people fell ill and 280 died, and one in Nzara, in what is now South Sudan (CDC 2026; WHO 2025). East Africa also met the disease long before 2014, since an outbreak of Sudan virus around Gulu, in northern Uganda, infected 425 people in 2000 and remained the largest Ebola outbreak on record until the West African epidemic (CDC 2026).

The name Ebola covers six related viral species, meaning six distinct kinds of virus within the same family, and three of them have caused large outbreaks in people: Ebola virus itself, often called Zaire virus after the former name of DR Congo; Sudan virus; and Bundibugyo virus (WHO 2025). All three cause the same illness, which begins suddenly with fever, exhaustion, muscle pain and a sore throat and can go on to vomiting, diarrhoea and failing kidneys and liver; across past outbreaks about half of all patients have died, although the share has ranged from 25 to 90 percent depending on the virus and on the care that patients received (WHO 2025).

The differences between the three viruses matter most for medicine, since the two approved vaccines and the two antibody treatments that exist were all developed against Zaire virus, and none of them is approved for use against Sudan or Bundibugyo virus (WHO 2025). Bundibugyo virus is also the least studied of the three, as it had caused only two outbreaks before this year: the first in 2007, in the Bundibugyo district of western Uganda, which WHO counted at 149 cases and CDC at 131,22 Counts of the same outbreak often differ between agencies, because each decides differently which suspected and probable cases to include and when to close the count. and the second in 2012, around Isiro in north-eastern DR Congo, where CDC counted 62 cases (Towner et al. 2008; CDC 2026; WHO 2025). The 2026 outbreak has already passed 11,000 confirmed cases, about 76 times the size of the first, and health workers are fighting it without a licensed vaccine or drug; WHO reports that a trial of candidate vaccines began enrolling volunteers in Bunia, the capital of Ituri, on 23 October (WHO 2026a).

Every outbreak starts with what scientists call a spillover, meaning the moment a person catches the virus from an infected animal, most likely through contact with the blood or meat of a fruit bat or of another wild animal (WHO 2025). Marí Saéz and colleagues traced the West African epidemic to a two-year-old boy in Meliandou, a village in southern Guinea, who fell ill in December 2013 and may have been infected while playing in a hollow tree that housed a colony of insect-eating bats (Marí Saéz et al. 2015).

Once the virus is in a person, it passes to others through direct contact with the blood or body fluids of someone who is sick or has died, or with bedding and clothing soiled by those fluids (WHO 2025). Three groups of people therefore face the highest risk of infection: relatives who nurse a patient at home, health workers who care for patients without full protective equipment, and mourners who wash and touch the body before burial. The eight people infected in Kenya, four relatives and four care workers, came from the first two of these groups.

Two features of the illness decide how an outbreak can be stopped: symptoms take between 2 and 21 days to appear after a person is infected, and according to WHO a person cannot pass the virus on until those symptoms appear (WHO 2025). The virus can still survive for months in some parts of the body after a patient recovers, particularly in semen, and Keita and colleagues showed that the 2021 outbreak in Guinea began this way, since the genomes of the virus taken from its patients sat within the family tree of the 2014 to 2016 virus and showed no sign of a new spillover from an animal (Keita et al. 2021).

What the storyboard shows, and what it hides

Our storyboard draws each outbreak since 2014 as a spike on satellite imagery, with the height of each spike set by its number of cases and its colour set by the virus that caused it, and it turns a spike grey once the outbreak is declared over; the dashboard holds the same outbreaks in a table that readers can filter by year, country, virus and status. Scrolled from start to finish, the storyboard’s spikes move from the Atlantic coast of West Africa to the Great Lakes region of East and Central Africa, and it is easy to read that movement as one disease travelling across the continent. Figure 1 sets the sizes of the largest outbreaks side by side, including the older ones that the storyboard leaves out.

Confirmed, probable and suspected cases: why the counts in Figure 1 are not quite alike

Health agencies sort patients into three groups. A confirmed case has tested positive in a laboratory; a probable case was never tested, often because the person died before a sample could be taken, but had the symptoms and a clear link to a confirmed case; and a suspected case has symptoms and a possible exposure but is still waiting for a result. The final totals for older outbreaks add the probable cases, and for West Africa the suspected ones as well, whereas the running count for 2026 includes confirmed cases only, so the 2026 bar in Figure 1 understates that outbreak when it is set against the others.

The West African epidemic remains the largest by far: between 2014 and 2016, Zaire virus spread from Meliandou through Guinea, Liberia and Sierra Leone, reached all three capital cities, and infected more than 28,600 people, of whom more than 11,300 died, according to CDC figures that include suspected and probable cases as well as cases confirmed in a laboratory (CDC 2026). Travellers and evacuated patients carried a small number of cases from the region to Nigeria, Mali, Senegal, the United States, Spain, the United Kingdom and Italy (CDC 2026).

The next large outbreak began in 2018 in North Kivu and Ituri, in eastern DR Congo, where fighting between armed groups often kept health workers from reaching patients and the people those patients had been in contact with; it lasted almost two years and ended with 3,470 cases and 2,287 deaths (CDC 2026). Smaller outbreaks followed in Guinea in 2021, in DR Congo, including 64 cases in Kasaï province in 2025, and in Uganda, where Sudan virus caused 164 cases around Mubende in 2022 and 14 in Kampala in 2025 (CDC 2026).

The Bundibugyo outbreak that began in Ituri in May 2026 has since spread to eight of DR Congo’s provinces and has crossed international borders three times that we know of: into Uganda, where 20 cases were confirmed before that chain of infection was declared over in August; into Kenya, with the traveller who flew to Nairobi; and to Nigeria, where a person who had travelled from Uganda fell ill in Abuja in October (WHO 2026a; ECDC 2026).

What the map hides is that the spikes in West Africa and the spikes in DR Congo are separate events, since each began with its own spillover from animals, years apart and thousands of kilometres from each other, and the outbreaks in Uganda and this year’s outbreak in DR Congo were caused by different viruses altogether. The only stretch of the line from west to east that is a single chain of infection, passed from one person to the next, is the last one, from Ituri to Nairobi, and starting the clock in 2014 makes the pattern look neater than it is, since Central and East Africa had already been dealing with Ebola for almost forty years by then.

The map is therefore best read as a record of where the virus has surfaced, and each time it surfaces, the same two things decide how large the outbreak becomes: how many people each patient infects, and how quickly health workers find and isolate those patients.

The arithmetic of an outbreak

Epidemiologists, meaning scientists who study how diseases spread through populations, summarise the growth of an outbreak with the reproduction number, written R, which is the average number of people that one infected person goes on to infect. If we call the first patient generation zero, the people that patient infects generation one, and the people they in turn infect generation two, then with each case infecting R others on average, the expected number of new cases in generation n is

E[Cn​]=Rn

When R is above 1, each generation is larger than the one before and the outbreak grows; when R is below 1, each generation is smaller and the outbreak shrinks until it runs out of people to infect. At the start of an outbreak, before anyone has responded, the value is written R0​ and called the basic reproduction number, and the WHO Ebola Response Team estimated it at 1.71 in Guinea, 1.83 in Liberia and 2.02 in Sierra Leone during the West African epidemic, meaning that each patient infected roughly two others before control measures took hold (WHO Ebola Response Team 2014). Once a response is under way, the value on a given day is written Rt​ and called the effective reproduction number,33 The small t stands for time: Rt​ is the value on day t, and it changes as people are isolated, treated or vaccinated. and health agencies most often estimate it from daily case counts with a method developed by Cori and colleagues (Cori et al. 2013).

The speed at which one generation follows another depends on the generation time, meaning the average gap between one person falling ill and the people they infect falling ill, which the WHO Ebola Response Team measured at 15.3 days in West Africa (WHO Ebola Response Team 2014). Wallinga and Lipsitch showed how the daily growth rate r and the doubling time Td​, meaning the number of days it takes for case numbers to double, follow from R and the generation time T (Wallinga and Lipsitch 2007):

r=TlnR​,Td​=rln2​

With R at 2 and T at 15.3 days, cases double every 15.3 days, which means sixteen times as many new cases after two months.44 Two months is about four generations of 15.3 days, and four doublings give 24=16. With R at 1.4, the doubling time stretches to about 31 days, close to the 30.2 days that the WHO Ebola Response Team measured in Sierra Leone in September 2014, when it estimated that the reproduction number there had already fallen from 2.02 to 1.38 (WHO Ebola Response Team 2014).

Code
# Daily growth rate and doubling time from R and the generation time
# (Wallinga and Lipsitch 2007); generation time from WHO Ebola Response Team (2014)
T_gen    <- 15.3
R        <- c(2, 1.4)
r        <- log(R) / T_gen
doubling <- log(2) / r
data.frame(R, r = round(r, 4), doubling = round(doubling, 1))
    R      r doubling
1 2.0 0.0453     15.3
2 1.4 0.0220     31.5

The same arithmetic explains why most introductions of the virus into a new place go nowhere. Blumberg and Lloyd-Smith describe how, if R is held below 1 where an infected traveller arrives, the expected size of the whole chain that the traveller starts, counting the traveller, is the sum of every generation (Blumberg and Lloyd-Smith 2013):

μ=1+R+R2+R3+⋯=1−R1​,R<1

With R at 0.5 the chain comes to two cases on average, and with R at 0.8 it comes to five. Chance also works in the response’s favour, since even with R at 2 and no response at all, about one introduction in five dies out by itself when the first patient happens to infect no one. Lloyd-Smith and colleagues showed that real outbreaks are even more uneven than these averages suggest, as most patients infect nobody while a few infect many; this pattern, called superspreading, makes a new introduction more likely to die out and makes the outbreaks that do take off grow faster (Lloyd-Smith et al. 2005).

Code
# Expected size of a chain started by one case when R is below 1
chain_size <- function(R) 1 / (1 - R)
chain_size(c(0.5, 0.8))

# Chance that a chain dies out on its own when R = 2, if the number of people
# each case infects follows a Poisson distribution: the smallest root of q = exp(R(q - 1))
q <- uniroot(function(q) q - exp(2 * (q - 1)), c(0, 0.99))$root
round(q, 3)
[1] 2 5
[1] 0.203

The cluster in Nairobi followed this pattern closely: one man infected up to eight people, far more than the average of two, and those eight infected nobody, since all of them were already in quarantine when they became able to pass the virus on, so the chain ended at nine.

Isolating patients works far better against Ebola than against influenza, and Fraser and colleagues showed that the reason lies in how much transmission happens before a person feels ill (Fraser et al. 2004). For Ebola that share is close to zero, so if health workers find and isolate a fraction c of cases soon after their symptoms begin, the reproduction number that remains is roughly

Reff​=R0​(1−c)

With R0​ at 2, the outbreak therefore shrinks once health workers promptly isolate more than half of all cases and the dead are buried safely. Influenza spreads before people know they are ill, so isolating the sick cannot do the same job against it, and Fraser and colleagues found that SARS and smallpox could be controlled in this way while pandemic influenza and HIV could not (Fraser et al. 2004).Fraser and colleagues write the share of transmission that happens before symptoms as θ. The formula above is their result with θ set to zero.

A vaccine adds a second line of defence by protecting people before they are exposed to the virus. In Guinea in 2015, the Ebola ça Suffit trial, reported by Henao-Restrepo and colleagues, vaccinated the contacts of each new case, and the contacts of those contacts, either at once or after a three-week delay; among the people vaccinated at once, nobody developed Ebola from ten days after their ring was enrolled, while the rings vaccinated after the delay had 16 cases (Henao-Restrepo et al. 2017). That vaccine was developed against Zaire virus, and no equivalent exists yet for Bundibugyo virus, which leaves the response teams in Ituri and North Kivu to rely on isolation, contact tracing and safe burial.

What the 2026 numbers say

Three groups have published estimates of the reproduction number for this outbreak, and together they suggest that it started at around 2 or a little higher and fell to around 1 within a few months; Table 1 sets them side by side.

The last two have not yet been peer reviewed, meaning that other scientists have not yet formally checked their methods, and none of the three covers October or November, so what follows is our own rough reading of the more recent case counts, which readers should treat as a sketch. Figure 2 shows how the confirmed cases and deaths reported by WHO and ECDC have added up since May.

DR Congo had 4,665 confirmed cases on 12 August and 9,277 on 14 October, an average of about 73 new confirmed cases a day over those nine weeks, and between 14 October and 15 November the count rose to 11,376, an average of about 66 a day (ECDC 2026; WHO 2026b). Nine weeks covers roughly four generations of Ebola, so if R had still been near 2, the daily figure would have doubled four times in that stretch and passed 1,000 by mid-October; Figure 3 shows that it stayed at about the same level the whole time instead, between 57 and 76 new confirmed cases a day since July.

A daily count that holds level for months is what an Rt​ close to 1 looks like, meaning that each case is replacing itself, so the outbreak is neither growing nor ending while about 60 to 70 more people test positive every day. WHO’s description in late October supports this reading, since it reported transmission continuing at a high level, with new cases in 50 of the 66 affected health zones over the previous three weeks and only about 80 percent of known contacts being followed up, against a target of 95 percent (WHO 2026a).A health zone is the basic unit of DR Congo’s health system, a district served by one general hospital and its clinics.

Three limits on the data, and why they call for caution

The figures count confirmed cases only, so the daily number can be capped by how many samples laboratories are able to test, and a flat line in Figure 3 might partly reflect a limit on testing as well as a steady outbreak. Reports also arrive late and are often revised. Finally, we have borrowed the 15-day generation time from Zaire virus in West Africa, since we have not found a published measure of it for Bundibugyo virus, and a shorter or longer generation time would change how many generations fit into nine weeks.

The death rate calls for the same care. On the reported figures, 5,568 of 11,376 confirmed patients have died, or 49 percent (ECDC 2026),55 Epidemiologists call this share the case fatality ratio: deaths divided by confirmed cases. against between 25 and 32 percent in Uganda in 2007, depending on whose count is used, and 55 percent around Isiro in 2012 (CDC 2026; WHO 2025), so this outbreak sits near the top of a very short record. A ratio taken in the middle of an outbreak can be wrong in both directions, however, since some recent patients have not yet recovered or died, and many people with mild illness are never tested.

Beyond Africa

Ebola has already left Africa this year: ECDC counts four cases linked to the outbreak in the European Union, made up of two patients flown to Germany and one to the Netherlands for specialist care, and a humanitarian doctor who fell ill after returning to France in July (ECDC 2026). All four had been working in health care or aid in the affected areas, and the patient in Nigeria was later flown to Switzerland, where the patient was discharged on 11 November, the same day that all 62 of the patient’s contacts in Nigeria finished their monitoring (ECDC 2026).

This year’s cases are in line with history, as van Zandvoort and colleagues found in June when they reviewed every known case of Ebola outside Africa since 1976: they counted 28 confirmed cases linked to African outbreaks, most of them health and aid workers flown home on purpose for treatment, and only four travellers who crossed a border feeling well and fell ill afterwards (van Zandvoort et al. 2026). Leaving out the planned evacuations, they put the rate since 2000 at about 0.17 cases outside Africa for every 1,000 cases reported in Africa, which, applied to this outbreak as a rough guide, gives

10000.17​×11,376≈1.9

On past form, then, we would expect about two unplanned arrivals on another continent by now, with roughly a one in seven chance of none, and there has been one, the humanitarian doctor who fell ill after returning to France.

Arriving is only the first step, since for Ebola to take hold somewhere new, the person who arrives has to infect others, and those people have to keep infecting others faster than health workers can find them; van Zandvoort and colleagues found that this has never happened outside Africa in fifty years of records (van Zandvoort et al. 2026).

The closest it has come was in Dallas, in the United States, in 2014, where, as Chevalier and colleagues describe, a man who had flown in from Liberia went to a hospital emergency department with a fever on 25 September and was sent home, came back by ambulance, was confirmed to have Ebola on 30 September and died on 8 October; two nurses who cared for him were infected and both recovered, and all 177 people traced as contacts of the three patients finished their 21 days of monitoring without another case (Chevalier et al. 2014). In the same year, a nursing assistant in Madrid caught the virus from a patient evacuated from Sierra Leone, the first time Ebola was known to have passed from one person to another outside Africa, and WHO reports that it went no further (WHO 2014).

Nairobi now belongs on the same list, and it stands out once health budgets are compared: World Bank figures show that in 2022 Kenya spent about 91 US dollars on health for each person, against about 2,950 dollars in Spain and about 12,600 dollars in the United States, so Kenya held its chain to nine cases while spending less than a thirtieth of what Spain spent per person and less than a hundredth of what the United States spent (World Bank n.d.). In all three cities, the first patient infected people who lived with or cared for that patient, and once health workers recognised the case, they cut the chain within one generation.

# Expected cases outside Africa on the historical rate (van Zandvoort et al. 2026)

Travel bans target the first step, arrival, and the evidence that they help is thin: Poletto and colleagues modelled the 2014 epidemic and found that even deep cuts in flights from the affected countries delayed the virus’s arrival in new countries by a few weeks at most (Poletto et al. 2014). Similarly, Bogoch and colleagues estimated that, without screening at departure, about 2.8 infected travellers a month would have boarded international flights from Guinea, Liberia and Sierra Leone, and they concluded that screening passengers as they leave, together with controlling the epidemic at its source, protects other countries far better than screening passengers when they arrive (Bogoch et al. 2015).

WHO’s advice this year follows the same evidence, as its updates in August and October rated the risk as very high inside DR Congo, high for the countries that share a land border with it and low for the rest of the world, and it advises countries against restricting travel or trade (WHO 2026a; WHO 2026b). ECDC, for its part, rates the likelihood of infection for people living in Europe as very low (ECDC 2026).

The risk people feel

Saudi Arabia closed its borders to travellers from Kenya over nine cases in a cluster that was already contained, and that decision shows how far the risk people feel can drift from the risk that case counts measure.

Researchers have measured that gap using Google Trends, a free tool that scores a search term from 0 to 100 according to its share of all searches made in a given place and period; according to Google, the score shows how much attention a topic received and says nothing about how many people searched or how worried they were (Google n.d.). Using this tool, Alicino and colleagues found that worldwide searches for “Ebola” in October 2014 were about 76 times higher than in March 2014, and that the single highest day, 16 October, came as President Obama called on National Guard reserves to help contain the virus (Alicino et al. 2015). Interest was highest in Liberia and Sierra Leone, where people were dying, while in the United States, Spain, the United Kingdom and Italy it was far lower and followed news coverage more closely than case counts. Towers and colleagues found the same pattern within the United States, where each Ebola news video was followed by tens of thousands of searches and tweets, although only a handful of people in the country were ever infected (Towers et al. 2015).

We have not yet repeated this analysis for 2026; we plan to compare searches in Kenya, Uganda and DR Congo with searches in the United States, the United Kingdom and France, and we will add the results to this article and to the dashboard.

Who carries the risk

Ebola will keep reaching other continents now and then, on a medical flight or with a returning aid worker, and fifty years of records show that a prepared hospital can stop it there; Nairobi has now shown that a hospital in Africa, working with far less money, can do the same.

The people who cannot count on that protection live in Ituri and North Kivu, where about 60 new cases are still being confirmed every day, about half of all confirmed patients have died, and the vaccines that might break the chain are only now entering trials. If Rt​ there is close to 1, as both the published estimates and the case counts suggest, the outbreak is balanced on what the response teams can manage from week to week, and a gap in funding or a new round of fighting could be enough to push it back into growth.

Everyone else’s safety is decided in Ituri and North Kivu as well, since a visa ban on travellers from Kenya does nothing to lower the daily count in Bunia, whereas money for tracing teams, isolation beds, safe burials and the vaccine trial does, and that spending protects people in Nairobi or Riyadh in the same way that it protects people in Bunia.

We will keep the storyboard and the dashboard updated as WHO and ECDC publish new figures.
